AGP: Alien Game Project

Multiplayer Extraction Prototype · Unity
Summer 2024Systems Designer & Gameplay ProgrammerTeam of 2Shelved
AGP

Overview

AGP, short for Alien Game Project, was a summer attempt at a Lethal Company-style co-op extraction prototype, built with the designer I usually work with. The concept was that players are refugees being hunted by their own mothership, forced to deploy, loot, and extract before the autopiloted ship leaves without them.

We shelved it midway through the summer. It still pushed me further technically than anything I had done before it, because it was my first real attempt at building a multiplayer gameplay space rather than experimenting with basic networked movement.

Goal
Build a co-op loop of deploying into a procedurally generated space, looting under pressure, and extracting together, with everything synchronized across players.

Learning multiplayer the hard way

This was where I first had to take multiplayer architecture seriously. I spent a lot of time learning how to properly synchronize player actions, manage shared game states, and decide when the server should have authority over an interaction rather than the client.

I implemented a physics-based player controller along with an interaction and inventory system supporting picking up and dropping items across the network, and picked up ScriptableObject workflows around the same time, using them to define item data. None of those systems are especially complex on their own. Combining them with multiplayer logic made everything significantly harder, and honestly it was more than I was ready to handle cleanly at the time. A lot of it turned into spaghetti.

Networking

Synchronization and authority

Shared game state across clients, and actual decisions about which interactions the server should own. This was my first real look at the difference between something working on my machine and something working for everyone at once.

Movement

Physics-based controller

A networked physics controller, which gave the loose and slightly chaotic feel that co-op games like this get a lot of value from. It was also where most of my synchronization problems came from.

Items

Networked interaction and inventory

Picking up and dropping items across the network, with item definitions moved into ScriptableObjects so new loot could be authored as data rather than code.

Sequences

Drop-pod and extraction

Deploying by drop pod and extracting on the returning ship, both synced for every player. These are the start and end of a run, so they are also the moments where things being out of sync would have been most obvious.

Procedural dungeon generation

The other half of the project was generation. I built a node-based dungeon generator that connected room prefabs through their doorways, accepting an array of rooms and randomly selecting and rotating them as it expanded outward from a starting room.

On top of that I wrote placement rules for the drop-off zones where players deploy. Generation guaranteed three of those rooms per dungeon and spaced them apart based on the seed, with one selected as the active insertion point each run. The starting room doubled as the extraction zone, where players could activate a beacon to call the ship in and deploy a platform for depositing items.

  1. Generate the dungeon and pick a seeded insertion point.
  2. Deploy the team by drop pod into that room.
  3. Loot outward through connected rooms under time pressure.
  4. Return to the starting room and activate the extraction beacon.
  5. Deposit items on the ship's platform before it leaves.

Why it stopped

It was ambitious for a two-person summer prototype with no dedicated programmer, and it never got to a polished state. What it taught me was pretty concrete though: multiplayer architecture, procedural systems, and the limits of trying to brute force everything on my own. If I go back to the idea I would rebuild it from scratch with cleaner systems and proper programming support, because I still think the concept is worth exploring.

Shelving it was the right call. I had already gotten what I was going to get out of it.
