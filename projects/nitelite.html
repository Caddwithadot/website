NiteLite

Overview

The NiteLite Engine was a custom 3D engine written entirely in C++ by the same team that previously built the 2D engine behind KnightLight, joined by two additional programmers. It included a custom C# scripting layer, which another designer and I used to build gameplay demos showcasing what the engine could do.

I joined during the second semester of my junior year while still working on the senior Knight Light project in Unreal. The plan was that by the time the Unreal project wrapped, NiteLite would be far enough along for us to start using it directly.

Goal
Stress the engine as its first real user, then build the demo that presents everyone else's prototypes in a single coherent space.

Being the engine's first user

A large part of my time went into playtesting engine features, reporting bugs, and documenting usability issues, plus suggesting missing features and workflow improvements from a designer's point of view. That mattered more here than it would on a commercial engine, because the programmers were building tools for an audience of two, and we were the two.

Working this way is also where I learned how much of a designer's speed is decided before they even open the editor. A missing inspector field or a confusing component name slows you down just as much as a bug does, and being the person who noticed those and wrote them down ended up helping the whole team, not just me.

The final demo

For the final showcase, the team built a hub-like lobby that let players walk between different prototype scenes. My main contribution was the player navigation and scene transition system holding it together.

I built a basic first-person controller and interactive doors that act as gateways between scenes. When the player walks up to a door an interact prompt appears, and opening it reveals a hallway that creates a non-Euclidean transition effect. I did that by carefully controlling when the new scene loaded and teleporting the player at the exact moment the camera was fully covered by the door surface, so you never see the swap and the space feels connected even though it isn't.

Navigation

First-person controller

Built inside the custom C# scripting layer instead of an established engine, which meant working without most of the conveniences I was used to and figuring out where the limits of the layer were.

Transitions

Non-Euclidean doors

Scene loads timed around when the camera is blocked, with the player teleported while the door surface covers the view. The hub feels like one connected space even though it's separate scenes.

Templates

Showcase room

A standardized exhibit room with a large viewing window and an interaction point that triggers the prototype and shifts the camera from first-person to a controlled presentation angle. Other team members used it as a drop-in template.

QA

Bug and usability reporting

Structured reporting on engine features, keeping actual bugs separate from workflow problems so the programmers could tell what was broken apart from what was just annoying to use.

What I took from it

This gave me real experience building gameplay systems on top of unfamiliar tools that were changing while I used them, and it made me better at giving feedback to the people building those tools. Designing inside something half-finished is uncomfortable, but it's also where a designer who can explain exactly why a workflow is painful is worth the most.

Explaining why a tool is hard to use is part of the design work, not just complaining about it.
