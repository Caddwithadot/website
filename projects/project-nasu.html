Project Nasu

Overview

Project Nasu was my first team project at DigiPen, during my freshman year. It's a 2D action-puzzle platformer built entirely in Unity using Visual Scripting, made by a team of three designers.

Because I had come from a technical school before DigiPen, I had a head start on the tools and naturally stepped into a leadership role. I set up the Unity project, introduced my teammates to source control, helped establish workflow, and ran SCRUM meetings to keep us on track. Since the rest of the team was still learning Unity, I also took on most of the implementation and effectively acted as lead programmer while still contributing to design.

Goal
Ship a complete platformer with a team still learning the engine, which meant building hazards our level designer could actually use without asking me to change anything.

Hazards as level design tools

I designed, prototyped, and implemented the player controller, collectibles, hazards, enemies, and a speedrun timer with time tracking. The hazards are the part I would still point at, because they were built specifically to give the level designer a flexible set of simple, readable tools rather than one-off obstacles.

Hazard 01

Red laser

Always on, but it can be disabled by shooting a button placed somewhere in the level. It turns a hazard into a puzzle by putting the off switch somewhere the player has to get to first.

Hazard 02

Green laser

Blinks on a fixed interval, creating timing windows the level designer could tune purely by placement and rhythm.

Hazard 03

Blue laser

Rotates within a 90 degree range, so it sweeps an area instead of blocking a line. It gave rooms a moving threat without needing to put an enemy in them.

Enemies

Rats and the mutated hamster

A rat patrolling between two points, a spawner that respawns rats after death, and a mutated hamster combining patrol movement with laser attacks, pausing at each turn point before firing horizontally.

Three lasers with clear, separate rules covered way more level design situations than a longer list of one-off hazards would have, and each one is easy to understand within a second of seeing it. The habit of building the flexible thing and letting content come out of it started here.

Running production

Alongside the implementation work I handled the production side, which meant project setup, getting everyone onto source control, establishing workflow, and running SCRUM meetings. Teaching two teammates source control while also writing most of the game is not a split I would recommend, but it did teach me early how much it costs a small team when the process isn't clear.

Recognition

Showcased in Foundry10's K-12 audio workshops and awarded a team honorarium, with the project also featured on DigiPen's Game Gallery and published on Steam.

What I took from it

This was my first real experience fully owning both design and implementation on a team, and it pushed me toward the kind of technical design work I still gravitate toward now. Most of what I have built since is a more developed version of what I stumbled into here.

Design one hazard well and the level designer can build twenty rooms out of it.
