Knight Light (3D)

Overview

During the first half of my junior year, another designer and I joined an existing senior team to help continue development on their project. Shortly after, that team merged with a second senior team and shifted to a different game, which happened to also be called Knight Light. It is entirely unrelated to the 2D KnightLight I had worked on the previous year, which is a coincidence I have given up trying to explain quickly.

This one is a 3D third-person action-adventure game built in Unreal, and I was brought on primarily for combat design and overall UX on a team of around 25 people.

Goal
Join a large project already in motion, identify what was making it feel unclear, and make it feel more cohesive without redesigning it out from under the original designers.

Starting with an audit

The project was in a rough state when I joined, so my first step was to play through the latest build and document everything I observed. I wrote detailed notes on what worked, what needed improvement, and what felt inconsistent or unclear, mostly from a UX and gameplay perspective, and turned them into a large feedback document that became the map of the project's problem areas.

The challenge from there was not to redesign the game. It was to refine and restructure what was already there so it felt more intentional, while still respecting what the original designers wanted. Joining a project late means the most useful thing you have is fresh eyes, and the fastest way to waste that is to propose a rebuild nobody asked for.

Turning findings into production work

The feedback document only mattered if it turned into work people actually did, so I created and maintained a large Design Requests spreadsheet that broke findings into actionable tasks across programming, art, audio, and VFX.

Each request carried context, reasoning, and implementation notes, so team members understood both the problem being solved and the outcome intended rather than just receiving a task. On a team of 25 that became a genuine communication tool: leads reviewed and prioritized requests by scope, and anyone could comment directly on an entry to ask for clarification or push back.

Documentation

Build feedback document

A full pass over the current build identifying UX and gameplay problem areas, written to be specific enough that someone else could act on any single entry without asking me what I meant.

Production

Design Requests spreadsheet

Tasks broken down across disciplines with the reasoning attached, reviewed and prioritized by the leads. This is what connected design intent to what actually got built.

Combat

Player and enemy design docs

Written proposals for improving player combat, reworking existing enemy behavior, and adding new enemy concepts that fit the game already there rather than the one I might have designed.

Balance

Tuning in Unreal

Direct changes to player and enemy variables plus a rebalance of the wave system. A lot of the time the fastest way to settle a design argument was to go into the engine, change a number, and play it.

Working at scale

This was by far the biggest team I have worked on, and it changed what being useful actually looked like. On a team of three, the limit is how much you can build. On a team of 25, the limit is whether everyone understands the same problem the same way, and most of what I contributed came from clearing that up rather than from anything I built myself.

Recognition

Featured on DigiPen's Game Gallery and released on Steam.

What I took from it

Stepping into a large project that was already running, looking at its problems objectively, and contributing in a way that made it more cohesive instead of starting over is a different skill than building something from nothing. It's also closer to what most actual jobs look like.

On a big team, a clearly written request that four people can act on is worth more than a feature only I could have built.
