KnightLight

Overview

KnightLight is a 2D puzzle-platformer built from scratch over two semesters by a seven-person team, where the programmers wrote a custom C++ engine while the designers built the gameplay inside it. I served as Design Lead, responsible for player controller design, core mechanic design and implementation, prototyping, playtesting, and helping steer the overall gameplay direction.

The engine did not exist yet when we started, so most of the first semester was spent prototyping in Unity, sometimes weekly, to test player abilities, lighting interactions, and movement feel while the programmers built in parallel. Those prototypes let us keep running playtests months before there was an engine to test in, and they became the foundation the final game was built on.

Goal
Deliver a cohesive, enjoyable puzzle experience inside a custom engine that was still being written underneath us.

Scoping down to one strong mechanic

The original concept was much larger. It had combat, enemies, collectible upgrades, and a metroidvania structure. As development went on and it became clear how much work the custom engine still needed, I helped push the decision to scale the project back and focus entirely on puzzle gameplay built around light.

That cut is what gave us the mechanic the rest of the game is built on. Instead of one character, the player is two connected parts: the Light, and the suit of armor it can climb into. Looking back, learning when to cut scope and focus on a smaller, more cohesive core was the most important lesson I took from the project.

Core mechanic: Light and Knight

The player primarily controls the Light, a small mobile entity that can slip through tight spaces and interact with puzzle elements. When the Light finds an abandoned suit of armor it can inhabit it and become the Knight, which changes what the player is able to do with the environment. The Knight is slower, but it is immune to hazards that would destroy the Light on contact, so progress depends on separating and recombining the two forms at the right moments.

Light

  • Fast and highly mobile
  • Fits through tight spaces the armor cannot
  • Illuminates dark areas and activates lamps
  • Destroyed by hazards on contact

Knight

  • Slower, but durable
  • Safely traverses spike hazards
  • Can launch the Light across larger gaps
  • Stays behind when the Light leaves

Most puzzles run on a simple loop, and nearly every space in the game is some variation of it:

  1. Explore as the Light to scout the space and activate lamps.
  2. Return to the armor and become the Knight to cross hazards safely.
  3. Separate again to reach what the Knight is too large or too slow to get to.

Later in the game the mechanic expands rather than being replaced: the Light gains the ability to launch upward as it exits the armor, which opens the same interaction into vertical traversal puzzles without asking the player to learn anything new.

Gameplay systems I designed and built

Core interaction

Flash ability

The Light emits a short burst of illumination to light dark areas and trigger lamps. This was one of the first mechanics I built and became the primary way players interact with every puzzle element in the game.

Progression

Lamp and door pairing

A tag-based system linking each lamp to a specific door. Flashing the lamp opens its paired door, which gave us clean cause-and-effect puzzles and let the level designer wire up progression without touching code.

Checkpointing

Torches and armor recall

Torches act as save points and as anchors for the armor. If the Knight ends up stranded somewhere the Light cannot return to, the player can recall the armor to the nearest lit torch, which turns checkpointing itself into a puzzle tool.

Traversal

Pipe network

A network system letting the Light travel through connected pipes using directional tags. The tags gave our level designer flexibility to build traversal puzzles and hidden routes out of the same components.

Collectibles

Signiflyers

Small firefly-like collectibles placed throughout the world. They give players something to find, but they also work as quiet guidance, giving you a distant point to move toward in the dark without an objective marker ruining the atmosphere.

Meta

Steam achievements

I implemented most of the achievements the team settled on, which meant building tracking hooks across several unrelated gameplay systems and making sure they fired reliably under conditions players could reach in any order.

Guidance and readability in the dark

The entire game takes place underground in near-total darkness, which made readability the design problem I spent the most time on. Players needed to identify hazards, interactables, and progression paths without the atmosphere being flattened by UI.

The clearest example is the spike hazards. Instead of making them always visible, I gave them a red glow that slowly gets brighter as the player gets closer, so the hazard warns you before it kills you without giving away the whole room from far away. It was a small addition, but it made the game feel a lot more fair while keeping the tension, and we ended up reusing the same glow for dialogue so players could tell what they could interact with. I also added a light floating dust effect around the player to keep the cave from feeling static.

Prototyping, then moving into the custom engine

Because the engine was still being written, every system started life as a Unity prototype. That let us test mechanics and puzzle interactions quickly and commit to them before anyone spent engine time implementing them. Once the engine stabilized in the second semester, I transitioned from prototyping to implementing mechanics directly inside it, adapting each design to the engine's own tools, components, and limitations.

This meant working closely and constantly with the programmers, since the engine's capabilities kept expanding underneath the gameplay. It was my first time designing extensively inside a custom engine, and it taught me more about designing within technical constraints than any project before it. To pass that on, I co-authored a 36-page Engine Design Guide with our other designer, documenting the workflows we used to prototype and implement gameplay so future designers would not have to rediscover them.

Playtesting

I ran playtests regularly across both semesters, recruiting participants, organizing sessions, and authoring 12 reports documenting player behavior, confusion points, usability issues, and technical problems. The feedback drove continuous refinement of the player controller, mechanic behavior, and how clearly systems communicated themselves.

The armor recall system is the best example of that working. Testing showed that players kept misunderstanding how recall functioned, so instead of explaining it with text I iterated on the visual and audio feedback until the mechanic explained itself.

Outcome

  • Released on Steam as a fully playable student project with 110+ positive reviews.
  • Highlighted on DigiPen's Game Gallery.
  • Completed over nine months by a seven-person multidisciplinary team.
  • Successfully transitioned gameplay from Unity prototypes into a custom C++ engine mid-development.

What I took from it

This was my first time being Design Lead on a team like this, and my first time designing gameplay inside an engine that was being written at the same time. It taught me how to manage scope, iterate on mechanics based on real player feedback, and design systems that were good but also actually possible within what the project could support.

Focusing everything around one mechanic is what let seven people finish something that felt whole. I don't think we would have finished the metroidvania version.
