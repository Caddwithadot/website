Strip Miner

Overview

Strip Miner was a summer Unity project with a team of three: myself, the designer I usually work with, and a programmer we had recently started working with. A Game About Digging a Hole had just come out, and we got talking about how much appeal a simple incremental digging game can have. That conversation turned into this.

The concept focused entirely on the act of strip mining, the way you would in Minecraft or Terraria. The player starts with a weak mining tool and gradually upgrades it by collecting and depositing mined voxels for credits, mining faster and more efficiently over time. The game was fully voxel-based, and the programmer spent most of his time building that foundation while I handled gameplay prototyping and system design.

Goal
Design a progression curve that starts frustratingly slow on purpose and ends with the player tearing through rock, without losing them in between.

The core loop

  1. Mine ore out of the voxel terrain with your current tool.
  2. Deposit what you collected for credits.
  3. Spend credits upgrading the tool for speed and efficiency.
  4. Push deeper into the next underground layer.

Each layer was designed as a self-contained slice of underground space, with progression driven entirely by the player's efficiency and upgrade choices rather than by unlocking a door or clearing a boss.

Designing the progression

My main role was gameplay prototyping and system design, while the other designer focused more heavily on UI. I implemented and tested early versions of the mining loop, deposit flow, and upgrade structure to check whether the progression actually felt worth continuing, which is the only question that matters in an incremental game.

I also wrote the full game design document, defining the upgrade systems, quota progression, helper items like explosives and ore-finding tools, and the overall roguelike layer structure.

Economy

Upgrade and credit flow

Mining feeds deposits, deposits pay for upgrades, and upgrades make you mine faster. The whole game is that loop speeding up, so tuning those numbers was most of the actual design work.

Pressure

Quota requirements

Targets the player has to hit, which gives the open-ended digging some structure and a reason to get efficient instead of just wandering around.

Tools

Helper items

Explosives and ore-finding tools as alternatives to just mining faster, so upgrading becomes a choice between different approaches instead of one straight line.

Structure

Depth-based layers

A roguelike layer structure where each depth unlocks new material and new pressure, keeping forward momentum tied to the fantasy of going deeper.

Pacing and motivation

The biggest design focus was pacing. Early progression is slow on purpose so that upgrades feel meaningful, but the goal was to eventually reach a point where the player tears through rock instantly and feels extremely powerful compared to where they started. Quota requirements, helper tools, and depth-based unlocks all existed to keep that sense of forward momentum going so the slow start didn't feel like a dead end.

What I took from it

The project never got past the prototyping phase and was never finished. Even so, it gave me a lot more experience designing long-term progression, because when a game is this simple mechanically there is nowhere for a weak economy to hide.

When the mechanic is basically one button, the progression curve is the whole game.
