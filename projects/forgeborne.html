Forgeborne

Exploring game feel through dynamic weapon formation

Forgeborne

Overview

Forgeborne is a five-week solo prototype built in Unity for DigiPen's DES365 Game Feel course. It's a first-person action experience built around a single unifying mechanic: electromagnetism. The player uses metal-manipulating technology to forge and test adaptive weapons in a junkyard arena, pulling scrap out of the air and reshaping it mid-fight.

The assignment asked for three weapons with three attacks each. My goal was to tie all nine together through one systemic interaction rather than designing nine isolated kits, so every weapon in the game is the same pile of shrapnel wearing a different shape.

Goal
Design three distinct weapons with three attacks each, all driven by one shared procedural mesh mechanic, while learning runtime mesh manipulation from scratch.

The procedural mesh system

To make one mechanic carry nine attacks, I built a runtime mesh-splitting system that takes any assigned model and breaks it into individual triangles to simulate metal shrapnel. Each triangle becomes its own GameObject, which is obviously not performance-friendly, but it was acceptable for a prototype and it made every weapon in the game physically the same material.

One system handles the mesh decomposition, and a second manages teleporting and reassembling the shrapnel behind the player. On top of that I built a reform animation pipeline where the pieces fly in, spin, and lock into place to reconstruct the original weapon model, then disperse again during attacks. That let me treat weapons as modular formations of scrap rather than static meshes.

  1. Start with the mesh of any model.
  2. Split it into individual triangles at runtime.
  3. Store the shrapnel behind the player, out of view.
  4. Fly, spin, and lock the pieces into the weapon's shape on demand.
  5. Disperse it again through the attack, then reform.

One mechanic, many outcomes

With the system in place, I designed three weapon archetypes. Each has a base form and three attacks, and every one of them reuses the same shrapnel foundation rather than introducing unrelated abilities.

Archetype 01

Melee

Built on a magnetic handle. The heavy attack forms a large hammer for a downward smash, the medium forges a spear for a thrust, and the light keeps the bare handle and delivers an electrically charged swing.

Archetype 02

Ranged

Base state is a magnetic pistol firing electric shots. The medium attack pulls in scrap to form a shotgun, and the heavy reconfigures the whole weapon into a sniper rifle with a stylized trickshot animation.

Archetype 03

Special

Leans into large-scale metal manipulation. The heavy summons two masses of scrap that slam together to crush what's between them, the medium forges an anvil in midair and drops it, and the light summons and throws a spear.

Best executions

Where it lands hardest

The hammer slam, the sniper trickshot, and the anvil drop are the three attacks that best capture the intended fantasy. The hammer's delayed, explosive impact in particular is the clearest proof the system was worth building.

The pivot

Originally I planned to handcraft each weapon out of primitive shapes that would lerp into formation, and I built a week-one prototype using stretched cubes to validate that magnetic assembly felt good before committing further. It did feel good, but after that first week it was clear the approach would never scale to nine attacks inside five weeks.

Moving to a procedural mesh-splitting solution was the biggest technical shift in the project and ultimately what made the concept viable at all. It also meant learning runtime mesh manipulation from scratch, mid-project, with the deadline already running.

What fell short

Most of my iteration time went into stabilizing and refining the electromagnetism system itself, which meant UI polish, audio, camera shake, and visual materials all stayed underdeveloped. Playtesting was limited too, and a lot of the feedback I did get centered on placeholder elements rather than combat feel, which cost me iteration opportunities late in development. Depth Strider, the prototype I built immediately after this one, is where I corrected that by prioritizing audio and ambience from the first week.

What I took from it

Despite the rough edges, this project reinforced a pattern I keep coming back to. I prefer building a flexible system first and then designing multiple mechanics that express that system in different ways, and I've used the same approach on nearly every project since, from the inspection stations in Line 9 to the object framework in Trash.

Even when the polish falls short, the systemic foundation is the part I care about most, and it's the part that keeps paying off in later work.
