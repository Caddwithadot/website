Jimby's Jumbo Jambo

Overview

Jimby's Jumbo Jambo is a silly little rhythm game set in Seattle, where Jimothy is oh so hungry and you help him grab as much food as his grubby little hands can get on the beat. It was made for Jimothy Jamothy, a game jam hosted by Seattle Indies where the theme was literally just Jimothy, Seattle's favorite little cryptid.

I teamed up with Jared Callupe Lopez again, and this time we had a whole crew. Jack Love helped on gameplay programming, Kaitlyn Giles did all of the art, and Derek Knight went above and beyond on sound, making the entire soundtrack including a vocaloid track with lyrics he wrote himself.

I was one of the game designers, and I spent most of my time on asset implementation and UX. In practice that meant owning Jimothy himself: building his rig, driving his motion from the song rather than from animation clips, and layering in the small details that make him read as a living thing instead of a sprite that moves.

Goal
Make the character sell the music. If Jimothy is convincing, the player feels the beat through him instead of reading it off the interface.

What I built

Rigging

Jimothy's rig

I created and hooked up Jimothy's entire rig, which is the piece everything else in his performance hangs off. Getting the hierarchy right early is what made the rest of the motion work cheap to add later in the jam.

Animation

Motion driven by the song

His animation is written programmatically rather than authored frame by frame, so his arms reach out, grab, and pull back based on what is actually happening in the song. Because the motion is generated from live state, it stays correct no matter how the track lines up.

Audio sync

MIDI-timed eating

The dynamic eating system reads its own dedicated lane in the MIDI file, so his chomps land in time with the music by construction rather than by hand-tuned offsets. Timing data lives with the song, which means a new track brings its own eating pattern with it.

Game feel

Small signs of life

On top of the big motions I spent a lot of time on the quiet stuff: idle bobbing, breathing, blinking, and a head that rocks to the beat. None of it is noticeable on its own, which is the point. Together it is the difference between a character and a prop.

Pipeline

Asset implementation

I handled getting art and audio into the build and wired up correctly, so the rest of the team could keep producing while integration stayed someone's actual job rather than an afterthought at the end of the jam.

Why drive it from the music

A rhythm game lives or dies on whether the thing on screen looks like it is hearing the same song the player is. Hand-authored animation would have been faster to get moving on day one, but it would have drifted the moment anything about the track or the timing changed, and it would have needed redoing for every new song.

Pulling the eating timing out of a MIDI lane meant the character and the music were reading from one source instead of two that had to be kept in agreement. On a jam schedule that is worth more than it sounds, because it removes a whole category of bug that only shows up when you are too tired to debug it.

What I took from it

This was the most character-focused work I have done. Most of my projects are about systems the player operates, and this one was about a thing the player watches, which puts all the weight on whether the motion is convincing rather than whether the rules are clean.

It also made the case for tying animation to data the game already has. The MIDI lane approach meant Jimothy's performance was a property of the song rather than a thing I maintained separately, and that is the part I would reach for again.

Nobody notices the breathing and the blinking on their own, which is exactly why they work.
